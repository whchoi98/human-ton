# English editing

Use these principles for English passages. In mixed documents, keep each passage's language and preserve shared terminology. Do not translate unless asked.

## Make the sentence easier to follow

Find the subject, action, and point. Unpack nominalizations when doing so clarifies them:

> The team conducted an evaluation of the migration plan.
>
> The team evaluated the migration plan.

Keep named procedures and terms of art intact. Prefer active voice when the actor is already known and relevant. “The records were deleted” must not become “The team deleted the records” unless the source names that actor.

Remove framing that merely announces the next sentence. Retain transitions that establish contrast, evidence, qualification, or sequence. Do not replace every repeated term with a synonym; technical consistency often matters more.

## Preserve the degree of the claim

“May reduce latency” is not “reduces latency.” “We plan to” is not “we will.” “Some respondents” is not “users.” “Not yet verified” is not “incorrect.” Preserve those distinctions even when the shorter alternative sounds more confident.

Keep direct quotations, numbers and their units, dates, product names, identifiers, code, formulas, citations, and link destinations unchanged. Check which population and condition each number describes, not merely that the digits survived.

## Rhythm without a formula

Use the information to decide where a sentence ends. Split a sentence when its nested qualifications obscure the main point. Join short sentences when their separation makes one idea feel disconnected. A series of short sentences can also be deliberate emphasis; leave it if it works.

Carry an established topic into the next sentence before introducing a new one when that improves continuity. Do not force every paragraph into topic sentence, three examples, and summary. Avoid adding a rhetorical question or grand conclusion merely to make the prose “engaging.”

## Em dashes and middle dots

Inspect repeated em-dash (—) interruptions. Split independent points into sentences or use commas or parentheses for a short aside when that makes the relationship clearer. Keep a purposeful dash that fits the author's voice. Do not add dashes merely to make a revision feel more expressive.

If middle dots (·) turn running prose into repeated label strings, use ordinary list punctuation or conjunctions without merging items or inventing relationships. Keep useful separators in headings and tables, and preserve quotations, names, code, mathematical expressions, and units such as `N·m`. Hyphens (-), range en dashes (–), and minus signs (−) are separate symbols with different roles. If the user explicitly requests removal, apply it to the requested prose outside protected spans.

## Keep the writer's voice

Use contractions only when they fit the source or requested register. Keep formality where it serves the audience. Conversational prose still needs complete meaning.

Do not invent a personal anecdote, emotion, sensory detail, or joke. Do not add spelling errors, slang, exaggerated informality, or irregular punctuation to simulate a human writer. A word such as “robust” or “leverage” is a prompt to inspect context, not proof of authorship or an automatic replacement rule.

Read the revision for clarity, then compare it to the original for meaning. If a sentence was already clear and the change only expresses the editor's preference, keep the original.
